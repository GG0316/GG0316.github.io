From Virtual Model to Physical Prototype:
Pterodactyl Robotic Skin Development

This portfolio presents my individual reflection on a group project to develop a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype. The project explores how a virtual Blender/CAD model can move towards a physical PETG 3D printed artefact, while also considering skin design, wing membrane materials, string-assisted movement, and future servo-based actuation. The website is written from my own perspective, so it focuses on what I learned about product development, design decisions, alternative methods, and future improvements.

The Product Development Journey

Project Overview and Individual Context

A group project presented through my own reflective product development narrative.

The group project aimed to develop a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin or exoskeletal prototype. The concept was chosen because the Pterodactyl has a strong visual identity and a natural connection to wings, membranes, and movement. This made it suitable for a module focused on robotic modelling and drone skin design, as the artefact required both an outer form and an internal development strategy.

At the time of writing, the prototype is in the final printing and preparation stage. This means the portfolio does not present the artefact as a completed production model. Instead, it presents the current development stage as part of a structured product development process. The evidence includes Blender/CAD designs, mechanism layouts, STL files, PETG 3D printing evidence, risk assessment work, skin and membrane planning, and my own research into wing movement methods.

My individual role focused on finding ways to make the Pterodactyl wings move. I investigated multiple options including motors, servo actuation, strings, elastic thread and material tension. Originally, the motor or servo route was the main direction because it offered controlled movement. However, after considering budget and time constraints, string-assisted movement became a more practical current option, while servo actuation remained the preferred future improvement.

Individual Position Statement

My portfolio focuses on the learning process rather than only the final group output. I reflected on how the design moved from virtual modelling into physical prototyping, how the group compared different manufacturing and skin options, and how my own movement research influenced the future direction of the wing mechanism. This helped me understand that product development is a staged process of selection, testing, revision and justification.

Product Development Process

The project was structured around five development phases: concept, design development, detailed development, preproduction and production planning.

1. Concept Design

High-level ideation, choosing the Pterodactyl form, and identifying the wing movement challenge.

2. Design Development

Mechanical ideas, material comparison, cost constraints, Blender/CAD modelling, and movement options.

3. Detailed Development

Refining files for fabrication, STL preparation, skin seams, access points, and print planning.

4. Preproduction

Physical samples, PETG 3D printing, risk assessment, support removal, and prototype evaluation.

5. Production Plan

Future workflow for final post-processing, skin application, movement testing and improved actuation.

1. Concept Design

The first phase was high-level ideation. The group selected a Pterodactyl because it gave the project a recognisable form and a clear wing movement challenge. From my perspective, the main concept question was how the wings could eventually move while still looking like a realistic Pterodactyl membrane.

Phase 1: Concept Design

High-level ideation and selection of the Pterodactyl as the main prototype direction.

The concept design phase was the starting point of the project. At this stage, the group considered the prototype from a high-level perspective rather than as a finished technical model. The Pterodactyl was chosen because it had a clear connection to wings, flight and membrane-based anatomy. This made it more suitable for robotic skin design than a static creature with fewer moving features.

Several early directions were possible. The group could have created a static display model, manually moved wings, motorised wings, servo-controlled wings, or string-assisted wings. A fully rigid 3D printed wing was also possible, but this would not represent the flexible membrane-like structure of a Pterodactyl as effectively. Therefore, the concept moved towards a body that could be 3D printed, with wings and skin planned as flexible or semi-flexible elements.

From my individual perspective, this phase helped me understand the importance of choosing a concept that has enough design potential. The Pterodactyl allowed the group to discuss form, structure, material, movement, access and maintenance. This meant the project could be evaluated as a product development process rather than only as a decorative model.

Phase 2: Design Development

Connecting the creative concept with mechanical design, cost and material decisions.

The design development phase connected the creative Pterodactyl idea with mechanical and practical considerations. Blender and CAD-based modelling were used to develop the body form, while the mechanism layouts explored possible support rails, moving arms and pivot positions. This stage was important because the prototype needed to work as both a visual dinosaur form and a future moving system.

Movement alternatives were also considered during this phase. A DC motor was one possible option because it can provide continuous rotation, but it would need additional linkages or control to convert that rotation into a repeated wing flap. A servo was more suitable from a technical point of view because it can move between controlled angles, which matches the up-and-down movement of a wing. However, budget and time constraints meant that string-assisted movement became more realistic for the current prototype stage.

Material alternatives were also compared. PLA was easy to print but more brittle. ABS could be tougher but is harder to print reliably. PETG was selected because it provided a practical balance: easier to print than ABS, less brittle than PLA, and more suitable for a handled prototype. This decision was especially relevant because the group discussed a possible school-style demonstration environment where durability and safety would matter.

Phase 3: Detailed Development

Refining the design for file-to-fabrication, skin planning and future maintenance.

The detailed development phase focused on turning the design into something that could be manufactured and maintained. This included preparing STL files, considering print orientation, support structures, overhangs and post-processing. It also included more detailed thinking about how the outer skin and wing membrane would attach to the frame.

A fully sealed skin was considered, but this would make internal mechanisms difficult to access. Instead, the group discussed selective adhesive points, seam lines, possible magnet panels and hidden screws. This would allow the skin to overlap naturally around areas such as the midsection while still allowing access for maintenance. This is important because any future wing movement mechanism may need to be adjusted, repaired or tested.

The wing membrane was planned as fabric or spandex with a thin silicone layer. Applying adhesive everywhere was rejected because it would make the structure inaccessible and could make the membrane too stiff. A more suitable solution was to apply adhesive at selected points and reinforce stress areas only where testing showed weakness.

Phase 4: Preproduction

Physical samples, PETG 3D printing, risk assessment and early prototype validation.

The preproduction phase is where the project moved from digital design into physical sampling. The PETG 3D print represents the main physical prototype evidence. At the time of writing, the prototype is still in the printing and final preparation stage, with the most recent print image included as evidence of the virtual-to-physical transition.

The value of this stage is that the design can now be judged as a real object rather than only as a model on screen. The printed artefact can be assessed for scale, surface quality, support material, strength, sharp edges and post-processing needs. These details are difficult to evaluate fully in Blender or CAD alone.

A risk assessment was also part of this phase. It identified hazards linked to 3D printing, post-processing and handling, including hot printer parts, heated beds, fumes, damaged cables, filament handling, sharp tools, support removal, trip hazards and sharp edges on the finished model. This showed me that physical prototyping requires safety planning as well as design planning.

Phase 5: Production and Future Production Planning

Using the current prototype as a foundation for a more refined functional version.

The production phase is presented as a future development plan rather than as a completed final product. The current prototype provides a foundation for a more refined version. A future production-ready iteration would include support removal, sanding sharp edges, applying a flexible spandex or fabric wing membrane, adding a thin silicone skin layer, using removable seams or access panels, and testing the wing movement system.

For the current prototype, string-assisted movement is the more achievable method because it reduces cost and complexity. For a future functional iteration, servo actuation would be preferred because it could provide more controlled and repeatable movement. The improved workflow would therefore test the string or servo mechanism separately before integrating it into the full Pterodactyl body.

Final Selected Workflow

The selected development workflow was: Blender/CAD virtual design → mechanism and skin planning → STL file preparation → PETG 3D printing → risk assessment and post-processing planning → spandex/fabric membrane with silicone skin planning → string and elastic thread movement option for the current prototype → servo actuation proposed for a future functional iteration.

This workflow was chosen because it matched the actual stage and constraints of the project. It also shows a clear path from virtual design to physical prototype, while still explaining how the design could develop into a more production-ready solution.

Skin, Membrane and Movement

Skin and Wing Membrane Design

Developing the outer skin strategy for realism, flexibility and access to internal mechanisms.

The skin design was important because the project was not only about producing a rigid dinosaur shape. It also needed to consider how an outer skin or membrane could sit over a mechanical structure. Silicone was considered as a possible skin material because it could create a more realistic surface finish and allow a flexible outer layer. For areas where direct silicone placement would be difficult, foam or a similar material was considered as an underlayer to smooth the body shape, round rough edges and support layer-by-layer skin application.

The wing membrane was planned as fabric or spandex with a thin silicone layer. Fabric or spandex could provide a lightweight flexible base, while silicone could improve realism and create a more skin-like finish. The membrane would wrap around the bone or frame edge once or twice, with adhesive applied only at selected points. Applying adhesive everywhere was rejected because it would make the structure inaccessible and could make the membrane too stiff.

The annotated skin plan shows different design considerations. The blue points represent possible adhesive locations for the membrane fabric, while the red lines represent possible seam positions for access. These seams could be supported by hidden screws or magnetic panels, especially around natural overlap areas such as the midsection. This was important because the internal mechanism would need to remain accessible for testing, adjustment and future servo integration.

Colour was also considered. Grey, brown and dull green tones were discussed because they would create a more natural Pterodactyl appearance. Tinting silicone could provide a realistic base skin tone, while painted details could be added later if the surface finish was suitable. Darker details around stress points could also make the skin look more realistic.

Skin Design Decision

The preferred skin strategy was a flexible membrane system using fabric or spandex, selective adhesive points, and a thin silicone surface layer. This was chosen because it supported the Pterodactyl wing shape while still allowing access to joints and internal mechanisms. Extra fabric reinforcement could be added at stress points if testing showed that the membrane was not strong enough.

String, Thread and Actuation Method Selection

Comparing movement options and selecting a practical current method with a stronger future route.

My individual contribution focused on investigating how the Pterodactyl wings could move. The movement options considered included a DC motor, servo motor, strings, shirring elastic thread, thin elastic cord, lycra or spandex elastic thread, thin rubber cord and normal sewing thread. Each option had different advantages and limitations.

A motorised route was attractive because it could make the prototype more interactive. A DC motor could provide continuous rotation, but this would require a linkage or crank system to convert the rotation into a flapping movement. A servo was more suitable for controlled wing movement because it can rotate between set angles. This matches the up-and-down motion needed for a flapping wing.

However, due to budget and time constraints, string-assisted movement became more practical for the current prototype stage. This decision was not simply a weaker option; it was a realistic response to the project constraints. A string-assisted system can demonstrate the movement concept with less cost, complexity and risk than a fully integrated motorised system.

For the thread material, I proposed spandex elastic thread as the most suitable choice because the wing skin was also planned to use spandex. Since both materials are stretchy, they should move together more naturally and allow the wing membrane to flex without becoming too stiff. The main limitation is tension: if the thread is too tight, it could make the wing resist movement; if it is too loose, the wing may not hold its shape properly.

Critical Appraisal

Techniques Used, Considered and Rejected

Each method is appraised for suitability in this project and wider industrial product development.

Blender, CAD and 3D Modelling

Blender/CAD and 3D modelling were selected as the main virtual prototyping methods. They were suitable because the Pterodactyl was designed from scratch and required control over body form, wing placement and mechanism layout. Blender was especially useful for organic modelling because the Pterodactyl body is not a simple geometric shape.

The limitation is that a digital model cannot fully prove physical performance. It cannot reliably show print quality, joint friction, material strength or how easily the final object can be handled. In wider industry, CAD and 3D modelling are essential for product development because they support visualisation, design communication, revision and manufacturing preparation. In this project, they were the correct starting point, but they needed to be followed by physical printing and testing.

PETG 3D Printing

3D printing was selected as the main physical prototyping method because it created a direct route from digital model to physical artefact. PETG was chosen because it offered a better balance of durability and printability than some alternatives. Compared with ABS, it is easier to print. Compared with PLA, it is less brittle and offers better impact resistance, temperature resistance and layer adhesion.

The limitation is that 3D printed parts still require support removal, post-processing and possible redesign. They may not be suitable for repeated mechanical movement without testing. In wider industry, 3D printing is valuable for rapid prototyping, custom components and low-volume production, but material selection and print orientation must be considered carefully.

Skin and Membrane Design

Spandex, fabric and silicone were considered for the outer skin and wing membrane. Spandex/fabric was suitable because it is lightweight and flexible, while silicone could improve realism and skin-like appearance. A fully rigid printed wing was rejected because it would be heavier and less realistic as a membrane.

A fully sealed silicone skin was also not ideal for the current prototype because it would make internal mechanisms difficult to access. Selective adhesive points, seams, possible magnet panels and hidden screws were preferred because they support maintenance. In industrial product development, this reflects a common issue: outer casing or skin must improve appearance and protection without preventing repair or inspection.

String-Assisted Movement and Servo Actuation

Several movement methods were considered. A DC motor could provide continuous rotation, but it would need additional mechanisms to convert rotation into flapping. A servo motor was more suitable for controlled angular movement because it can move between set positions. This made servo actuation the preferred future method for wing movement.

For the current prototype, string-assisted movement was more practical because it reduced cost, complexity and time. Spandex elastic thread was proposed because it would work with the spandex wing skin and allow flexible movement. The limitation is that string tension must be controlled carefully. In wider robotics, both cable-driven mechanisms and servo systems are useful, but they require careful testing of friction, load, torque and repeatability.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was considered but not selected as a main method. It would have been useful if the group had first created a clay, foam or hand-made Pterodactyl body and needed to convert that physical model into a digital file. Its strength is capturing existing organic shapes quickly.

It was not suitable for the current project because the Pterodactyl was created digitally from the beginning. There was no finished physical object to scan. In industry, 3D scanning is valuable for reverse engineering, quality inspection, medical modelling, heritage preservation and custom-fit products.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was considered in relation to wing movement, but it was not selected for the current stage. The planned movement was a simple string or servo-assisted wing flap, not a detailed biological motion study. Motion capture would have added complexity without directly improving the first physical prototype.

In a future research version, motion capture could be useful for analysing real bird or bat wing movement and comparing it with the mechanical wing path. In industry, motion capture is used in robotics, biomechanics, animation, game development and autonomous system testing where accurate movement data is required.

Haptic Sculpting

Haptic sculpting was considered as an alternative for creating organic dinosaur features such as the head, body surface, wrinkles or skin texture. It would be useful where tactile feedback helps a designer sculpt complex curved forms.

It was not selected as the main method because the current stage focused on manufacturable geometry, mechanism layout and 3D printing rather than high-detail surface sculpting. It would be more suitable in a later aesthetic refinement stage after the body, movement system and access panels have been tested.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was considered for flat support pieces, wing ribs, rails or lightweight frame components. It is accurate and repeatable for sheet materials, so it could be useful for making symmetrical internal structures.

It was not selected as the main method because the Pterodactyl body and mechanism required more organic 3D geometry. However, laser cutting could be useful in a later version for producing a lightweight frame under the spandex membrane. In industry, laser cutting is widely used for sheet material manufacturing, signage, packaging, architectural models and precision fabrication.

Resin Production and Composite Methods

Resin printing and composite methods were considered as future development options. Resin could improve the detail and surface finish of parts such as the head or claws. Composite methods could create stronger and lighter wing or body shells.

They were not selected for the current prototype stage because they require more post-processing, workshop control and preparation. For the first physical iteration, PETG 3D printing was more direct and practical. In industry, resin and composites are useful where high surface finish, durability or lightweight strength are required, such as aerospace, automotive, medical modelling and product design.

Critical Appraisal Summary

The project did not use every technique explored in the module, but each technique was considered in relation to suitability. Blender/CAD, STL preparation and PETG 3D printing were selected because they best supported the current virtual-to-physical prototype stage. Spandex, fabric, silicone, seams and string movement were selected because they supported a practical skin and movement strategy. 3D scanning, motion capture, haptic sculpting, laser cutting, resin and composite methods were appraised as alternatives or future options rather than ignored.

Individual Reflection

What Worked Well

The virtual-to-physical route was clear. Blender/CAD created the design, STL files prepared it for fabrication, and PETG printing produced physical evidence. The process also included material, skin, movement and risk planning.

Main Limitations

The prototype is still in the printing and preparation stage, so full movement testing is not yet complete. String tension, servo torque, spandex behaviour, seam access and post-processing still need practical testing.

Future Improvements

A future version should test a small wing rig first, then refine the string or servo mechanism, adjust membrane tension, improve access panels and apply the skin after mechanical testing.

This project helped me understand that product development does not move directly from idea to final production. The virtual model gave the group a clear design direction, but the physical print provided different information about scale, supports, surface finish, post-processing and handling. This showed me that virtual and physical prototypes should work together as part of an iterative process.

My movement research showed that actuation is not only about choosing a motor. The wing material, string tension, joint friction, cost, time and access to the mechanism all affect whether the movement system is realistic. A servo is technically suitable for controlled wing movement, but string-assisted movement is more achievable for the current prototype. This helped me understand that design choices must respond to project constraints.

The skin planning stage helped me understand that a prototype can fail even if the mechanical idea is good. If the skin is permanently attached, the mechanism may be difficult to repair. If too much adhesive is used on the membrane, the wing may become stiff and inaccessible. Therefore, selective adhesive points, seams, possible magnetic access panels and reinforced stress points were more practical design choices.

If I developed the project further, I would first print smaller test pieces before committing to the full body. I would then build a small wing test rig to compare string-assisted movement and servo actuation. After testing, I would refine the CAD model, add stronger pivot points, test spandex tension, apply silicone only after mechanical access had been confirmed, and use removable seams or panels for maintenance.

The Pterodactyl project reached a valuable preproduction prototype stage. It produced Blender/CAD models, mechanism layouts, STL files, PETG 3D printing evidence, risk assessment work and skin/membrane planning. From my individual perspective, the most important learning outcome was understanding that prototyping methods should be selected based on suitability. The project demonstrates a clear route from virtual prototype to physical artefact, while also identifying a realistic future workflow for wing movement and robotic skin development.
