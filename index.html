Development Journey

Individual Context

The whole website is written as a reflective development record, not just a final project report.

This project aimed to develop a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype. I understood the concept to be suitable because the Pterodactyl has large wings, a membrane-like structure and clear potential for movement. The prototype is currently best understood as a pre-production development stage rather than a fully finished production model.

My reflection focuses on what I explored and learned during the process. I reviewed how the design moved from Blender/CAD modelling into STL preparation and PETG 3D printing. I also explored wing movement methods, skin materials, elastic thread, roller-guided string routing, cost awareness, risk assessment and future improvements.

1. Concept Design

Understanding why the Pterodactyl was a suitable robotic skin concept.

At the concept design stage, I understood that the Pterodactyl was a strong choice because it gave the project more than a visual shape. Its wings created an opportunity to think about membrane materials, movement, internal structure and robotic skin design. A static dinosaur model would have been easier to complete, but it would not have allowed the same level of development thinking.

The early alternatives included a static display model, a fully rigid 3D printed wing, manual wing movement, a motorised wing mechanism, a servo-controlled system and a string-assisted system. I found the flexible wing concept more suitable because it connected directly to the idea of a robotic skin and allowed me to think about how materials behave when movement is introduced.

What I Learned at This Stage

This stage helped me realise that the project needed to be treated as a product development process, not only as a modelling task. From the beginning, I was thinking about how the wings could move, what material would allow movement, and how the outer skin could remain realistic without blocking access to the mechanism.

2. Product Development

Turning the concept into a more practical design through modelling, material selection and movement planning.

During product development, I reviewed how the Blender/CAD model supported the movement from concept to manufacture. The digital model helped define the Pterodactyl body, wing position and available space for future internal mechanisms. This showed me why virtual modelling is useful before physical prototyping.

I compared several movement options. A DC motor could provide rotation, but it would need additional linkage to turn that rotation into a wing flap. A servo was more suitable for controlled movement because it can rotate between set angles. However, because of the limited development time before submission, the current design moved towards a roller-guided string system, with servo actuation kept as a future improvement.

Development Sketches and Roller-Guided String System

The development sketches helped me understand how the movement idea evolved from simple string pulling into a more controlled roller-guided system. A direct string pull could create friction or uneven movement if the string rubbed against the body or wing frame. The roller system was therefore a more practical idea because it could guide the string path and reduce friction.

This was one of the most useful learning points for me. I realised that a movement system is not only about the force used to pull the wing. The path of the string, the alignment of the rollers, the attachment points, and the tension all affect whether the final movement will be smooth or unreliable.

Material Selection and Cost Awareness

I also considered the material choice. PLA would have been easy to print but more brittle. ABS could be stronger but harder to print reliably. PETG was selected, and I understood this as a suitable choice because it offers a better balance of printability, durability and impact resistance for a handled prototype.

Cost was not the main limitation, but recording cost was useful as part of product development. The current prototype cost was recorded as £22.10, including the 3D printed prototype, spandex wing membrane, glue and PETG filament. Silicone was recorded as £0.00 for the current stage because it was considered as a future skin improvement rather than used in the present prototype.

Skin and Wing Membrane Planning

The skin planning stage helped me understand that the outer surface of the prototype affects both appearance and function. Spandex or fabric was suitable for the wing membrane because it is lightweight and flexible. A thin silicone layer could improve realism, but it could also add weight or reduce access if applied incorrectly.

I learned that a fully sealed skin would not be suitable for an early prototype because the internal mechanism may need to be adjusted. For that reason, selective adhesive points, seams, hidden screws or magnetic panels were more practical than permanently sealing the model.

String, Thread and Actuation Method Selection

I compared a DC motor, servo motor, simple strings, roller-guided strings, shirring elastic thread, thin elastic cord, lycra/spandex elastic thread, thin rubber cord and normal sewing thread. I proposed spandex elastic thread because the wing skin was also planned to use spandex. Since both materials are stretchy, they should move together more naturally.

The key limitation is tension. If the thread is too tight, it could resist movement and make the wing harder to pull. If it is too loose, the wing may not hold its shape properly. This helped me understand that actuation is not only about choosing a motor; it also depends on string routing, roller alignment, membrane tension and maintenance access.

3. Pre-Production

Moving from virtual design into PETG 3D printing, risk assessment and physical evaluation.

The pre-production stage helped me understand the difference between a virtual prototype and a physical prototype. In Blender/CAD, the model can look suitable, but the physical print reveals practical issues such as support structures, surface quality, sharp edges, post-processing and handling strength.

The PETG print was useful because it gave physical evidence of the design moving beyond the screen. It also made the risk assessment more meaningful, because hazards such as heated printer parts, fumes, support removal and sharp edges only become relevant once the design is physically manufactured.

The risk assessment identified hazards linked to printer setup, heated parts, fumes, damaged cables, filament handling, support removal, eye protection, sharp tools and finished model handling. This helped me understand that physical prototyping requires safe planning as well as design decisions.

What I Learned at This Stage

Pre-production showed me that a design is not ready just because the digital model looks complete. The physical print must still be assessed for safety, strength, surface finish, post-processing and whether it can support the planned movement and skin system.

4. Post-Production and Future Development

Planning the finishing, movement testing and next functional version.

The post-production stage is currently a planned next step rather than a fully completed stage. Once the print is complete, the model would need support removal, sanding, inspection and possible reinforcement before the wing membrane is attached.

If I developed the project further, I would first test the roller-guided string system on a smaller wing rig before applying the final skin. This would allow me to check string tension, roller alignment, membrane resistance and wing movement before sealing or covering the mechanism. I would only add silicone skin layers after confirming that the internal mechanism remains accessible.

Future finishing could include spandex or fabric wing membrane attachment, selective silicone coating, seam refinement, possible magnetic access panels and final colouring. Grey, brown and dull green tones were considered because they would give the prototype a more natural Pterodactyl appearance.

Final Selected Workflow

Blender/CAD design → mechanism and skin planning → STL preparation → PETG 3D printing → risk assessment and post-processing → spandex/fabric membrane with silicone planning → roller-guided string movement for the current prototype → servo actuation as a future improvement.

Critical Appraisal

Methods Used, Considered and Rejected

This section reflects on each method based on suitability for this prototype and wider product development.

Blender, CAD and 3D Modelling

Blender/CAD was suitable because the Pterodactyl needed controlled digital development before manufacture. Blender supported organic shaping, while CAD-style modelling supported structure and fabrication planning. I learned that digital modelling is useful for form and planning, but it cannot fully prove print quality, strength, friction or handling.

In wider industry, CAD and 3D modelling are essential for visualisation, revision, communication and manufacturing preparation.

PETG 3D Printing

PETG 3D printing was suitable because it created a direct route from digital model to physical artefact. I understood PETG to be a good choice because it is easier to print than ABS, less brittle than PLA, and suitable for a handled prototype.

The limitation is that printed parts still need support removal, post-processing and inspection. In industry, 3D printing is useful for rapid prototyping and custom parts, but material choice and print orientation are important.

Roller-Guided String Movement and Servo Actuation

A DC motor was considered, but it would require extra mechanisms to create flapping. A servo was preferred for future movement because it can move between set angles and provide controlled actuation.

For the current prototype, a roller-guided string system was more realistic because of the limited development time before submission. This improved the basic string idea because rollers can guide the string path, reduce friction and make movement smoother.

The limitation is that the system still depends on careful string tension, roller alignment and secure attachment points. In wider robotics, cable and pulley systems are useful when force needs to be transferred through a compact structure.

Skin and Membrane Design

Spandex, fabric and silicone were considered for the skin and wing membrane. Spandex/fabric was suitable because it is lightweight and flexible, while silicone could improve realism. A fully sealed silicone skin was not ideal because it would block access to the mechanism.

I learned that the outer skin must support appearance without preventing repair. In wider product development, access panels, seams and modular covers are often needed where internal systems require maintenance.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was considered but not selected. It would have been useful if a clay or hand-made Pterodactyl body needed to be digitised. It was less suitable here because the design started digitally.

In industry, 3D scanning is useful for reverse engineering, inspection, medical modelling, heritage work and custom-fit products.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was considered in relation to wing movement, but it was not needed for the current roller or servo-assisted flap concept. A future version could use it to compare the wing path with biological movement.

In industry, motion capture is used in robotics, animation, biomechanics and movement analysis.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was considered for flat ribs, rails or frame pieces. It was not selected as the main method because the Pterodactyl body required organic 3D geometry. It could still be useful later for lightweight internal wing ribs.

Resin and Composite Methods

Resin and composites were considered as future options. Resin could improve detail and surface finish, while composites could create lighter and stronger structures. They were not selected because PETG 3D printing was more direct and practical for this stage.

Appraisal Summary

The selected workflow was suitable because it matched the actual development stage. Blender/CAD supported virtual design, PETG 3D printing supported physical prototyping, spandex and silicone planning supported the robotic skin, and the roller-guided string system created a realistic current movement method. Servo actuation remains the stronger future improvement.

Final Reflection

Key Learning

A short final reflection, because the main reflection is already built into each stage of the website.

Overall, this project helped me understand that a prototype is not only judged by whether it is finished, but by how clearly the development process is understood. The Blender/CAD model helped plan the form, but the PETG print made practical issues such as support removal, surface finish, sharp edges and post-processing much clearer.

My biggest learning was that wing movement depends on more than the actuator. At first, I focused mainly on motors and servo movement, but I later realised that string routing, roller alignment, membrane tension, friction and access points all affect whether the movement can work reliably.

If I developed the project further, I would build a small wing test rig first. I would use it to compare the roller-guided string system with servo actuation, test the spandex membrane tension, check friction in the rollers and only apply the final silicone skin once the movement and access points were confirmed.
