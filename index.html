The Portfolio

An individual reflection on the development of a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype.

Why We Chose a Pterodactyl:
Skin, Structure and Wing Movement

Our group discussed several possible ideas before selecting the Pterodactyl. I felt this was a suitable direction because its wing structure connected naturally to the assignment theme of robotic skin, movement and physical prototyping. It allowed me to think about flexible membrane materials, internal support, string routing, roller-guided movement and future servo actuation. This individual reflection portfolio explains what I explored, what decisions made sense, and what I learned during the process.

Development Journey

How the portfolio is organised around the four main stages of the project.

This portfolio follows the project through four stages: concept design, product development, pre-production, and post-production/future development. Each stage explains what was explored, what alternatives were considered, why certain decisions were suitable, and what I learned from reviewing or contributing to that part of the process.

2. Product Development

4. Post-Production

1. Concept Design

Exploring possible project directions and deciding why the Pterodactyl was suitable.

At the concept design stage, different creature and movement ideas were discussed before the Pterodactyl direction was selected. I understood the Pterodactyl to be a strong choice because it was not only visually interesting, but also gave the project clear development challenges: large wings, a flexible membrane, an outer skin, internal support structure and possible movement.

At this stage, the useful comparison was between different ways of achieving movement, structure and skin behaviour. A fully rigid printed wing could have made the structure easier to control, but it would not represent the flexible membrane of a Pterodactyl very well. A flexible wing membrane was more suitable because it created a stronger link between the creature concept, robotic skin design and future wing movement.

The Pterodactyl also created a useful design problem because the wing is not separate from the rest of the body. The body shape, the wing frame, the membrane tension and the internal movement path all influence each other. This made the concept more interesting to analyse because each choice created another question: how would the skin attach, where could the string run, how much resistance would the wing membrane create, and how could the internal mechanism remain accessible?

My Reflection

This stage helped me understand that a good concept should support the technical aims of the assignment. The Pterodactyl was suitable because its wings naturally created questions about movement, flexible membrane behaviour, skin attachment, material tension and future actuation. This made the concept useful for exploring the route from virtual design to physical prototype.

2. Product Development

Comparing design, material, skin and movement options before deciding the current prototype direction.

The product development stage was where the concept became more practical. The project explored Blender/CAD modelling, 3D printing material choice, skin research, risk planning and wing movement. My main contribution focused on motors, servo actuation, strings and elastic thread options, while the wider design decisions helped me understand how each part affected the overall prototype.

This was the stage where I began to realise that the prototype could not be developed as separate pieces. The body, skin and wing movement all depended on one another. If the wing membrane became too stiff, the movement system would need more force. If the string path was poorly aligned, the wing could move unevenly. If the skin was fixed too permanently, the internal mechanism would become difficult to adjust. These connections made the development stage the most important part of the project for my learning.

Blender/CAD Modelling

Blender Body Development Video

This video shows the digital model being reviewed before physical manufacturing. It supports the product development stage because it shows how the virtual form was checked before moving towards 3D printing.

Blender/CAD modelling was used to develop the Pterodactyl body and wing form before physical manufacture. The advantage of this approach was that the shape could be viewed, adjusted and prepared digitally before committing to a print. Blender was suitable because it allowed more organic forms, which suited the Pterodactyl body better than simple block-based modelling.

Other digital routes were also considered. 3D scanning would have been useful if there was already a hand-made body or clay model that needed to be converted into a digital file. Haptic sculpting could have helped with surface texture, wrinkles and more natural skin detail. However, the current project began with digital modelling, so the priority was to produce a manufacturable form that could move towards 3D printing and mechanism planning.

My Reflection

Reviewing the digital design helped me understand why virtual prototyping matters. It allowed the body shape, wing layout and possible mechanism space to be considered before printing. However, I also learned that a digital model alone cannot prove whether the movement, material tension or post-processing will work in reality.

Movement Development: String, Roller Routing and Future Servo Actuation

The movement idea started with the need to make the wings move in a simple but controllable way. A string-based system was explored because it could transfer pulling force to the wings without making the prototype too complex at this stage. As the design developed, rollers became important because they could guide the string through the body more smoothly and reduce friction.

My main contribution was researching possible ways to support this wing movement, especially through motors, servo actuation, strings and elastic thread. A DC motor was considered as one possible actuator, but it would need an additional crank or linkage to convert continuous rotation into a wing-flapping motion. This would add more parts, more alignment issues and more chances for friction or failure.

A servo motor was more suitable for the future version because it can move between set angles, which matches the upstroke and downstroke of a wing. However, the servo should not be treated as separate from the string and roller system. The stronger idea was that a future servo could pull or control the string through the guided roller path, rather than forcing the wing directly.

Several ways of routing and controlling the string were considered, including the eye system, the locking cuff method and the roller system. The eye system could keep the string in position, but it may create more rubbing at the guide points. The locking cuff method could secure the string, but it may make adjustment harder if the tension needs to be changed. The roller system was selected because it gave the string a smoother and more practical path, while still allowing a servo to be added in a future version to control the pulling movement.

My Reflection

This was one of my main learning points. At first, I focused mainly on the actuator, such as whether a motor or servo would be suitable. I then realised that the actuator is only one part of the movement system. The string path, roller position, friction, tension and attachment points all affect whether the wing can move smoothly. The final roller system made more sense because it improved the string route first, which is important before adding servo control in a future version.

Thread and Wing Support Options

As part of my individual contribution, I also looked into different thread and string options for supporting the wing membrane. The options included shirring elastic thread, thin elastic cord, lycra/spandex elastic thread, thin rubber cord and normal sewing thread.

Normal sewing thread would be easy to attach and simple to source, but it has very little stretch. That could make the wing too stiff or place more load on the movement system. Thin rubber cord would provide stretch, but it may be harder to control neatly and could look less integrated with the wing membrane. Shirring elastic and thin elastic cord were possible options because they are flexible, but I felt spandex elastic thread matched the wing skin better.

I proposed spandex elastic thread because the wing skin was also planned to use spandex. Since both materials are stretchy, they should move together more naturally and allow the wing membrane to flex. The limitation is that the tension must be carefully controlled. If it is too tight, it could resist movement; if it is too loose, the wing may not hold its shape.

My Reflection

This helped me understand that small material choices can affect mechanical performance. The motor, servo, string and roller path are only part of the movement. The thread, membrane and tension all influence whether the wing can move smoothly.

3D Printing Material Selection

The project also compared possible 3D printing materials. PLA, ABS and PETG were considered. PLA would have been easy to print and commonly available, but it is more brittle and may crack or shatter more easily. ABS can be stronger and more temperature resistant, but it is harder to print reliably and usually needs more controlled printing conditions.

PETG was selected for the current prototype because it offered a better balance between printability and durability. It is easier to print than ABS while being tougher and less brittle than PLA. This made it suitable for a handled prototype, especially because the model may need post-processing, demonstration and further development. The trade-off is that PETG can sometimes require more careful post-processing than expected, but for this project its toughness and handling strength were more valuable than producing a very fine surface finish immediately.

My Reflection

Reviewing the material decision helped me understand that filament choice is part of product development. The selected filament affects strength, safety, handling and post-processing, not just the appearance of the printed model.

Skin and Wing Membrane Planning

The project also explored different skin and membrane options. A fully rigid printed wing would have been easier to keep in shape, but it would not behave like a flexible Pterodactyl membrane. Fabric or spandex was more suitable for the wing membrane because it is lightweight and flexible. A thin silicone layer was considered as a future improvement because it could make the surface look more realistic.

The disadvantage of silicone is that if it is applied too heavily or used to seal the model completely, it could add weight and make the internal mechanism difficult to access. For that reason, selective adhesive points, seam lines, hidden screws or magnetic panels were considered more practical than permanently sealing the skin. This was important because an early prototype needs to remain adjustable while movement and tension are still being tested.

My Reflection

The skin planning connected directly to my movement research because the wing material affects how hard the mechanism has to work. A skin that looks realistic but is too stiff or too tightly attached could make the wing movement unreliable.

3. Pre-Production

Moving from virtual design into physical printing and safety planning.

Pre-production involved preparing the design for physical manufacture. The main route selected was PETG 3D printing because it allowed the digital model to become a physical prototype. Remaining only at the virtual stage would not reveal practical issues such as support structures, sharp edges, surface finish, post-processing needs or handling strength.

The advantage of 3D printing was that it gave a real object that could be inspected and developed further. The limitation was that the printed part still required post-processing, including support removal, sanding and checking for weak or sharp areas. This showed that printing is not the end of the process; it is the beginning of physical evaluation.

The risk assessment helped identify hazards linked to printer setup, heated parts, fumes, damaged cables, filament handling, support removal, sharp tools and finished model handling. This made the physical stage more realistic because it considered not only whether the prototype could be made, but whether it could be handled and developed safely.

My Reflection

This stage helped me understand the difference between a virtual prototype and a physical prototype. In Blender/CAD, the design can look complete, but the physical print shows the real problems: supports, edges, surface finish, strength, access and safety. I also realised that any future movement system has to be planned around the physical body, not just the digital design.

4. Post-Production and Future Development

Planning finishing, movement testing and the next functional version.

Post-production is currently a planned next step rather than a fully completed stage. The first stage would be removing supports, sanding sharp edges and improving the surface. This would make the model safer and more presentable, but it would still need movement testing before the outer skin is fixed permanently.

A stronger future direction would be to test the selected string and roller path on a smaller wing rig before attaching the final skin. This would allow string tension, roller alignment, membrane resistance and wing movement to be tested without risking damage to the full prototype. After that, servo-controlled string actuation could be explored as a more repeatable movement method.

For the final skin finish, spandex or fabric could be attached first, followed by selective silicone coating if needed. The advantage of silicone is realism, but the disadvantage is that it could reduce access or add stiffness. Grey, brown and dull green tones were considered because they would give the prototype a more natural Pterodactyl appearance.

Final Selected Workflow

Blender/CAD design → mechanism and skin planning → STL preparation → PETG 3D printing → risk assessment and post-processing → spandex/fabric membrane planning → selected roller-guided string path for the current prototype → servo-controlled string actuation as a future improvement.

My Reflection

If I developed the project further, I would not apply the final skin immediately. I would first test the wing mechanism separately, then adjust the string path, roller alignment and membrane tension. This would reduce the risk of covering the mechanism too early and later needing to damage the skin to fix movement problems.

Critical Appraisal

Methods Used, Considered and Rejected

This section reflects on each method based on suitability for this prototype and wider product development.

Blender, CAD and 3D Modelling

Blender/CAD was suitable because the Pterodactyl needed controlled digital development before manufacture. Blender supported organic shaping, while CAD-style modelling supported structure and fabrication planning. The main advantage was that the form could be changed digitally before printing. The limitation was that a digital model cannot fully prove print quality, joint friction, material tension or handling strength.

In wider product development, CAD and 3D modelling are valuable because they allow designers to revise, communicate and prepare designs before committing to physical manufacture.

PETG 3D Printing

PETG 3D printing was suitable because it created a direct route from digital model to physical artefact. PETG was selected because it offered a practical balance between printability, toughness and durability. Compared with PLA, it was less brittle. Compared with ABS, it was easier to print reliably.

The limitation is that printed parts still need support removal, post-processing and inspection. In wider industrial applications, 3D printing is useful for rapid prototyping and custom parts, but material choice, print orientation and finishing still affect performance.

String-Roller Movement and Future Servo Actuation

The movement approach was based on a string-guided system supported by rollers. The aim was to create a path where the string could pull the wing with less friction and better alignment. A servo was considered as the future actuator because it could pull or control the string through set angles, creating a more repeatable wing-flapping motion.

Different routing ideas were explored before the selected roller system. The eye system could guide the string but may increase friction at the contact points. The locking cuff method could help hold or secure the string, but it may make tension adjustment harder. The selected roller system was more suitable because it gave the string a smoother path and was easier to connect with future servo actuation.

The main limitation is that the system still depends on accurate roller alignment, careful string tension and secure attachment points. In wider robotics and product development, cable, pulley and roller-guided systems are useful when force needs to be transferred through a compact structure, but they must be tested carefully to reduce friction and uneven movement.

Skin and Membrane Design

Spandex, fabric and silicone were considered for the skin and wing membrane. Spandex/fabric was suitable because it is lightweight and flexible, while silicone could improve realism. The trade-off is that silicone can add weight, stiffness and access problems if applied too heavily.

The selected direction was to keep the skin accessible through selective adhesive points, seams or removable panels. This helped balance appearance with maintenance and mechanism access.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was considered as an alternative route from physical form to digital model. It would have been useful if a clay, foam or handmade Pterodactyl body had already existed and needed to be digitised.

It was less suitable for this project because the design began digitally. In wider industry, 3D scanning is useful for reverse engineering, inspection, heritage work, medical modelling and custom-fit products.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was considered in relation to wing movement, but it was not needed for the current roller or servo-assisted flap concept. A future version could use motion capture to compare the prototype wing path with biological wing motion.

In wider industry, motion capture is valuable in animation, biomechanics, robotics and movement analysis, but it would have added unnecessary complexity at the current prototype stage.

Haptic Sculpting

Haptic sculpting could have helped create organic surface detail, such as skin texture, wrinkles or more natural body features. It was more relevant to aesthetic refinement than to the current mechanical and manufacturing stage.

For this project, the priority was the route from CAD/Blender to physical printing and movement planning. Haptic sculpting could become more useful later if the prototype moved towards a more realistic final surface.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was considered for flat ribs, rails or frame pieces. Its advantage is accuracy and repeatability for sheet materials. However, the Pterodactyl body required organic 3D geometry, making 3D printing more suitable for the main structure.

Laser cutting could still be useful later for lightweight internal wing ribs or support plates under the membrane.

Resin and Composite Methods

Resin and composites were considered as future options. Resin could improve surface detail and finish, while composites could create lighter and stronger structures. The trade-off is that they require more careful workshop control, post-processing and planning.

For the current stage, PETG 3D printing was more direct and practical. Resin or composite methods would be more suitable for a later refined version.

Appraisal Summary

The selected workflow was suitable because it matched the actual development stage. Blender/CAD supported virtual design, PETG 3D printing supported physical prototyping, spandex and silicone planning supported the robotic skin, and the selected string and roller system created a realistic current movement method. Servo-controlled string actuation remains the stronger future improvement.

Final Reflection

Key Learning

A final reflection on the main lessons from the development process.

Overall, this project helped me understand that a prototype is not only judged by whether it is finished, but by how clearly the development process is understood. The Blender/CAD model helped plan the form, but the PETG print made practical issues such as support removal, surface finish, sharp edges and post-processing much clearer.

My biggest learning was that wing movement depends on more than the actuator. At first, I focused mainly on motors and servo movement, but I later realised that string routing, roller alignment, membrane tension, friction and access points all affect whether the movement can work reliably.

If I developed the project further, I would build a small wing test rig first. I would use it to compare the selected string and roller system with servo-controlled string actuation, test the spandex membrane tension, check friction in the rollers and only apply the final silicone skin once the movement and access points were confirmed.

The most important lesson I took from the project is that movement, material and access are connected. A wing mechanism cannot be designed without thinking about the membrane, and the skin cannot be applied without thinking about maintenance. This changed how I understand prototyping: the purpose is not just to make something look complete, but to reveal what needs to be tested and improved next.
