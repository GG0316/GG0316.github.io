The Product Development Journey

Project Overview and Individual Context

A group artefact, presented through my individual product development reflection.

The project involved the design and development of a Pterodactyl dinosaur prototype with wings that could flap using a servo motor. Although the artefact was produced as part of a group project, this portfolio is written from my individual perspective. My reflection focuses on how the concept moved from an initial design idea into a virtual prototype and then into a physical artefact, while also considering the suitability of the tools and methods explored during the module.

The Pterodactyl was chosen because its wing structure gave the group a clear movement challenge. Unlike a static dinosaur model, a Pterodactyl has large wings, a recognisable body form, and a natural connection to flight. This made it suitable for a robotic modelling and drone skin design context, where the external form, lightweight structure, and movement mechanism all had to be considered together. The prototype did not aim to create a flying drone. Instead, it aimed to demonstrate how a biologically inspired outer body could be connected with a simple mechanical actuation system.

Individual Position Statement

My individual focus was to understand how the design decisions affected the final prototype. I considered the balance between visual appearance, mechanical movement, weight, material suitability, and ease of assembly. This meant thinking beyond the final model and asking whether the workflow would be practical if the artefact had to be improved, reproduced, or developed into a more production-ready robotic skin.

Design Requirements and Success Criteria

The prototype needed to be visually recognisable, mechanically simple, and suitable for demonstration.

Recognisable Pterodactyl Form

The outer shape needed to clearly communicate the dinosaur concept. This influenced the long wing span, body profile, head shape, and overall silhouette. The visual design was important because the artefact needed to work as a portfolio piece as well as a mechanical prototype.

Servo-Based Wing Movement

The main functional requirement was that the wings should flap using a servo. A servo was appropriate because it can move to controlled angles rather than spinning continuously. This made it easier to create repeated up-and-down wing movement.

Virtual-to-Physical Workflow

The project needed to show a journey from digital or conceptual design into a physical artefact. This meant that sketches, CAD screenshots, model images, and build photos were important evidence of development rather than decoration.

Material and Weight Awareness

Wing movement is affected by weight. Heavy wings would demand more torque from the servo and increase stress on the joints. Therefore, lightweight materials and a simple linkage were more appropriate than a heavy, highly detailed sculptural model.

Assembly and Maintenance

The model needed to be assembled in a way that allowed the servo and wing joints to remain accessible. If the mechanism failed during testing, it would be difficult to repair if the servo was fully hidden inside a sealed body.

Reflective Evaluation

The final success of the project was not measured only by whether the wings moved. It was also measured by how well the development decisions could be justified and how clearly the limitations could be identified for future improvement.

1. Concept Selection

The group selected a Pterodactyl because it provided a strong visual identity and a clear mechanical challenge. The large wings allowed the project to demonstrate movement, while the dinosaur form connected the artefact to biomimicry and exoskeletal design. From my perspective, this was a suitable concept because it required both creative modelling and engineering judgement.

Virtual Prototyping

Using digital design to reduce uncertainty before physical construction.

The virtual prototype stage was important because it allowed the group to think through the form of the Pterodactyl before committing to physical materials. Computer Aided Design and 3D modelling are valuable in product development because they support visualisation, proportion checking, and technical planning. In this project, the virtual stage helped identify the main areas that would affect the physical build: wing span, body size, servo location, joint clearance, and the relationship between the decorative skin and the moving internal mechanism.

From my individual perspective, the most important value of the digital model was not simply that it looked like a Pterodactyl. Its value came from allowing the group to check whether the wings could move without colliding with the body. A visually attractive wing shape would have been unsuccessful if it blocked the servo linkage or placed too much load on the moving joint. This showed me that virtual prototyping is strongest when it is used as a decision-making tool rather than only as a presentation image.

One limitation of the virtual stage was that it could not fully predict the behaviour of the physical materials. For example, the stiffness of the wing, the looseness of the joint, and the actual strength of adhesive connections could only be understood properly during assembly and testing. This created a need for iteration. The digital prototype provided a useful plan, but it did not remove the need for physical experimentation.

Design Decision

The group prioritised a simple flapping mechanism over a complex multi-joint wing. This decision was appropriate because the project required a demonstrable prototype within limited time and resources. A more realistic wing mechanism may have improved biological accuracy, but it would also have increased the risk of mechanical failure, weight problems, and assembly difficulty.

Physical Prototyping and Assembly

Turning the digital idea into a working physical artefact.

The physical prototype stage changed the project from a design concept into an artefact that could be handled, tested, and demonstrated. This stage revealed practical issues that were less obvious in the virtual model. These included the strength of joints, the effect of wing weight, the alignment between the servo horn and the wing pivot, and the need to keep the body stable while the wings moved.

The prototype needed a balance between appearance and function. If the body was made too detailed or too heavy, it could make the model more attractive but reduce the reliability of the wing motion. If it was made too simple, it would be easier to build but less convincing as a Pterodactyl. This trade-off is a common issue in product development because prototypes often need to demonstrate function before they can be refined aesthetically.

In my view, the physical build was the stage where the product development process became most reflective. Small decisions had a visible effect on the model. For example, changing the wing angle or reducing wing weight could improve movement more effectively than adding more power. This showed that mechanical efficiency is not only about the actuator; it is also about designing the surrounding structure so the actuator is not overloaded.

Interactive Servo Wing-Flapping Demonstration

Move the slider to simulate how servo angle affects wing position.

The servo mechanism is the functional centre of the prototype. A standard hobby servo is suitable for this kind of demonstrator because it can rotate to controlled positions. In the Pterodactyl model, this controlled angular movement can be translated into wing motion through a direct connection, linkage, or pivot arrangement. The most important mechanical aim is to create repeated movement without placing excessive stress on the servo shaft or the wing joint.

The wing simulator above is a simplified visual representation. It does not calculate actual torque or material stress, but it helps communicate the design principle: as the servo angle changes, the wings move up and down around a pivot. This makes the portfolio more interactive and helps the viewer understand the relationship between control input and mechanical output.

In the real prototype, smooth flapping depends on the alignment of the mechanism. If the servo is not centred, the wings may move unevenly. If the linkage is too short, movement may become sharp and uncontrolled. If the linkage is too long, the wing movement may become weak or limited. Therefore, the mechanism required testing rather than only theoretical planning.

Critical Appraisal

Appraisal of Prototyping Techniques

Techniques used and not used are evaluated for this project and wider industrial use.

CAD and 3D Modelling

CAD and 3D modelling were highly suitable for the Pterodactyl project because they allowed the group to visualise the body and wings before making the physical artefact. A digital model can be inspected, scaled, revised, and presented more easily than a hand-built prototype. For this project, CAD thinking was especially useful for checking the relationship between the body, wings, and servo mechanism.

The weakness of CAD is that it can create a false sense of certainty. A digital part may look accurate on screen but still fail physically if the material is weak, the joint is loose, or the servo lacks enough torque. Therefore, CAD was suitable as an early development method, but it needed to be supported by physical testing.

In industry, CAD is essential because it supports precision, collaboration, technical documentation, and manufacturing preparation. However, the level of CAD detail should match the prototype stage. Early concepts may benefit from quick, simple models, while production-ready designs require more accurate tolerances, assemblies, and simulation.

3D Printing / Additive Manufacturing

3D printing is useful when a project requires custom forms that would be difficult to cut by hand. For the Pterodactyl, it could be suitable for the head, body shell, wing joints, servo mounts, or decorative features. Its main advantage is that complex geometry can be produced directly from digital files.

The main limitation is material behaviour. FDM printed parts can show layer lines and may be weak in certain directions depending on print orientation. Thin wing sections may flex or snap if not designed carefully. Printing can also take time, especially if multiple iterations are needed. For this reason, 3D printing is effective for custom parts but may not be the best method for every part of a lightweight flapping-wing model.

In industrial product development, additive manufacturing is valuable for rapid prototyping, design iteration, tooling, and low-volume production. However, it must be selected with awareness of cost, material performance, surface finish, print time, and post-processing.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was suitable to consider for flat wing ribs, body panels, or lightweight structural pieces. It is accurate, repeatable, and efficient for sheet materials. If the Pterodactyl wings were designed as flat layered components, laser cutting could produce consistent left and right wing profiles.

The limitation is that laser cutting mainly works with flat sheet geometry. It is less suitable for organic curved surfaces such as a dinosaur head or body unless the design is built from layers or interlocking panels. It can also create burnt edges on some materials and requires safe ventilation.

In industry, laser cutting is useful for signage, packaging, sheet-metal prototyping, architectural models, and manufacturing fixtures. For this project, it was more suitable for structural and flat parts than for sculptural dinosaur details.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was explored as a module technique, but it was not essential for this prototype because the Pterodactyl was a designed object rather than an existing object that needed to be copied. If the group had built a clay model first, scanning could have helped convert the hand-made shape into a digital file.

The advantage of 3D scanning is that it captures real-world geometry quickly. It is useful when a designer needs to reverse engineer an object or preserve organic detail. The limitation is that scan data often requires cleaning, smoothing, and retopology before it can be used for manufacturing.

In industry, scanning is valuable in heritage preservation, medical modelling, manufacturing inspection, reverse engineering, and custom-fit products. In this project, it was less suitable than CAD because the design needed to be created and controlled rather than captured from an existing object.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was also not a primary method for the Pterodactyl prototype. The wing motion was simple and servo-driven, so it did not require capturing complex biological movement. However, motion capture could be useful in a more advanced version of the project if the goal was to compare the prototype wing motion with bird or bat wing movement.

The strength of motion capture is its ability to record movement accurately over time. It can help engineers analyse motion paths, joint behaviour, and performance. Its limitation is that it requires special equipment, calibration, controlled space, and data processing.

In industrial and research contexts, motion capture is useful for robotics, animation, biomechanics, sports science, and autonomous systems testing. For this project, it was valuable as a concept to appraise, but not necessary for the basic flapping-wing demonstrator.

Resin Production and Composite Methods

Resin printing and composite methods could improve the quality and strength of a more advanced Pterodactyl prototype. Resin printing can produce fine surface detail, which would suit the head, claws, and decorative dinosaur features. Composite methods could create lightweight but strong wing skins or body shells.

The limitations are cost, safety, time, and post-processing. Resin parts often require washing and curing, while composite methods require careful handling, mould preparation, and workshop control. These methods may be too time-consuming for an early educational prototype unless the final surface quality is a major priority.

In industry, resin and composites are important where lightweight strength, surface finish, and durability are required. In this project, they are more appropriate as future development methods than as the first choice for a simple servo-driven demonstrator.

Reflection and Improvements

What Worked Well

The project worked well as a demonstrator because the moving wings made the prototype more engaging than a static model. The Pterodactyl theme gave the artefact a clear identity, and the servo mechanism provided a simple way to show controlled robotic movement.

Main Limitations

The main limitations were mechanical accuracy, wing balance, and material strength. A simple mechanism can demonstrate movement, but it may not produce natural flapping. The prototype also depends heavily on careful alignment between the servo and the wing joints.

Future Improvements

Future improvements could include lighter wing materials, stronger pivot joints, a better servo mount, smoother linkage geometry, and a more refined body shell. A second prototype could also use CAD simulation or motion analysis to improve the wing path.

This project helped me understand that virtual and physical prototypes should not be treated as separate stages. The virtual model helps with planning, but the physical prototype reveals real constraints. The most useful workflow is iterative: design, build, test, revise, and then improve the design again. I learned that a prototype does not need to be perfect to be valuable; it needs to reveal information that helps the next decision.

The servo was appropriate because the prototype required controlled angular motion rather than continuous rotation. This made the wing movement easier to demonstrate. However, the servo also introduced limits. If the wing was too heavy or the joint too stiff, the servo could struggle. This showed that actuator selection must be considered alongside material and structural decisions.

If I repeated the project, I would create a clearer test plan earlier. I would test a small wing and servo linkage before finalising the body. This would reduce the risk of designing a visually successful model that is difficult to animate. I would also document each design change with photos, short notes, and version numbers so that the development process is easier to explain in the final portfolio.

The Pterodactyl prototype successfully connected creative modelling with a simple robotic movement system. The project demonstrated how a virtual design can be developed into a physical artefact, but it also showed that physical testing is essential for understanding movement, material behaviour, and assembly constraints. From my individual perspective, the most important learning outcome was understanding how design choices must be justified in relation to function, manufacturing method, cost, time, and future improvement.
