The Product Development Journey

Project Overview and Individual Context

A group project presented through my own reflective product development narrative.

The project explored the development of a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype. The wider group aim was to create a dinosaur-like artefact that could eventually support wing movement, using a combination of virtual design, physical prototyping, and mechanism planning. Although the final artefact belongs to the group project, this portfolio is written from my individual perspective and focuses on what I learned about moving from virtual prototypes to physical prototypes.

The current prototype stage is best understood as an early product development iteration. The group created CAD and 3D model designs, developed STL files for mechanism parts and support rails, and produced a 3D printed wing or mechanism component. The project also investigated material and actuation decisions for a later functional version. In particular, spandex was selected as the preferred wing skin material because it is lightweight and flexible, while servo actuation was identified as the most suitable future mechanism for controlled wing flapping.

From my individual perspective, the most important learning point was that prototype development is not only about producing a finished object. It is a process of exploring alternatives, comparing methods, testing partial solutions, and justifying why certain techniques are selected over others. The project therefore became a useful case study in product development decision-making, even though the current iteration is not a fully actuated final model.

My individual reflection focused on how the Pterodactyl idea could move from a virtual CAD concept towards a physical prototype. I considered the suitability of CAD, 3D modelling, 3D printing, spandex wing skin, and servo actuation. I also compared these choices with alternative techniques introduced during the module, including 3D scanning, motion capture, haptic sculpting, laser cutting, resin production, and composite workshop methods.

1. Concept Direction

The group selected a Pterodactyl-inspired concept because it had a strong visual identity and a natural connection to wing movement. From my perspective, this concept was suitable because it required both creative modelling and mechanical thinking. It was not enough for the artefact to look like a dinosaur; it also needed a realistic development path towards movement and physical prototyping.

Virtual Prototyping: CAD and 3D Modelling

Using digital design to explore the Pterodactyl form before physical manufacturing.

The virtual prototype stage was the foundation of the project. The CAD model allowed the group to explore the overall Pterodactyl form, including the body, head, wing or arm layout, and general proportions. This was important because the prototype needed to look visually recognisable while also allowing space for mechanical development.

From my individual perspective, CAD and 3D modelling were the most suitable starting methods because the Pterodactyl was being designed from scratch rather than copied from an existing object. The digital model allowed the design to be controlled, adjusted, and discussed before any material was used. It also created a clear route into 3D printing because parts could be exported as STL files for manufacturing.

The limitation of the virtual model was that it could not fully prove physical performance. A design can look correct on screen but still create problems when manufactured. For example, a wing or mechanism part may become too heavy, too weak, or difficult to assemble once printed. This showed me that CAD is strongest when it is used as part of an iterative process rather than as a final answer.

Design Decision

CAD and 3D modelling were selected as the main virtual prototyping methods because they allowed the Pterodactyl form and mechanism layout to be developed intentionally. This was more suitable than 3D scanning because there was no finished physical object to capture. The design needed to be created and controlled from the beginning.

Mechanism Exploration

Early digital layouts for support rails, moving arms, and future actuation.

The mechanism layouts show an early exploration of how separate parts could support movement. The red and grey components represent possible arms, rails, and support structures that could later be linked to an actuator. This stage was useful because it connected the visual Pterodactyl concept with the practical requirements of a moving model.

From my individual perspective, the mechanism exploration was important because it showed that a robotic skin or dinosaur form cannot be designed only from the outside. The internal structure and movement path also affect the success of the prototype. A visually attractive body would not be useful if the mechanism could not fit inside or connect to the wing structure.

The mechanism shown in the CAD images should be understood as an exploratory prototype rather than a final production-ready system. Its purpose was to help evaluate possible support points, movement paths, and component relationships. In a later iteration, these parts would need to be tested with actual servo movement, stronger joints, and a defined wing linkage.

Physical Prototyping: 3D Printed Component

Turning selected STL designs into physical evidence for evaluation.

The physical prototype stage created the clearest link between virtual and physical development. A 3D printed wing or mechanism component was produced from the digital design. This allowed the group to assess how the CAD geometry translated into a real manufactured part.

The value of the printed component was that it made the design easier to evaluate. On screen, a part can look suitable, but a physical print reveals practical issues such as scale, surface finish, rigidity, thickness, and handling. These are important factors for a future moving prototype because any part linked to a servo or wing mechanism must be strong enough to function but light enough to avoid overloading the actuator.

3D printing was selected because it offered a direct route from CAD to physical artefact. It was suitable for producing custom shapes without needing moulds, casting, or complex workshop preparation. However, it also has limitations. A printed part may require sanding, revision, or redesign before it is suitable for repeated movement. For that reason, this stage should be treated as physical validation rather than a final production solution.

Material Selection: Spandex Wing Skin

Choosing a flexible membrane rather than a fully rigid printed wing surface.

For the wing skin, the group decided to use spandex as the preferred flexible material. This was selected because the Pterodactyl wing needed a lightweight membrane rather than a fully rigid surface. Spandex is suitable because it can stretch over a wing frame, create a smooth skin-like appearance, and add less weight than thicker fabric or solid printed material.

This material decision was important because any future servo-actuated wing movement would be affected by the weight and resistance of the wing surface. A fully 3D printed wing could provide a fixed shape, but it would increase mass and place more load on the actuator. Spandex better represents the membrane-like form of a Pterodactyl wing while also supporting the practical aim of keeping the moving structure lightweight.

The limitation of spandex is that it must be tensioned carefully. If it is too loose, the wing may lose its shape and look unfinished. If it is too tight, it may resist movement and increase stress on the frame or servo linkage. Therefore, a future version would need a suitable frame, ribs, or elastic thread support to control the shape of the membrane.

Material Decision

The final material direction combined rigid and flexible thinking: 3D printed parts were suitable for mechanism, rail, or support components, while spandex was more suitable for the wing skin. This combination allowed the prototype to be considered as a robotic skin system rather than only a static 3D printed dinosaur model.

Proposed Servo-Based Wing Actuation

Servo control was selected as the preferred future mechanism for wing flapping.

0°

As part of the mechanism development, I investigated servo actuation as the preferred method for creating wing-flapping movement in a future functional iteration. A servo motor was considered suitable because it can rotate to controlled angles. This is more appropriate for flapping movement than a continuously rotating DC motor because the wing needs to move between defined upper and lower positions rather than spin continuously.

The current prototype stage focused on CAD development, mechanism layout, 3D printed component validation, and material selection. Full servo integration would be the next functional stage. This is still a valid product development route because it separates form validation from actuation testing. First, the shape and components are developed; then the mechanism is tested; then the final moving assembly can be refined.

The main issue with servo actuation is torque. A Pterodactyl wing can create load because the wing is long compared with the body. Even if the wing skin is lightweight spandex, the servo must still overcome the distance from the pivot, friction in the joint, and resistance from the stretched material. Therefore, a future iteration should test servo strength, linkage length, wing weight, and pivot friction before the mechanism is fixed inside the body.

Critical Appraisal

Alternatives Considered and Final Method Selection

Techniques used and not used are evaluated in relation to the Pterodactyl prototype and wider industrial use.

CAD and 3D Modelling

CAD and 3D modelling were selected as the main virtual prototyping methods. They were suitable because the Pterodactyl was being designed from scratch and needed controlled digital development. CAD allowed the form, scale, wing layout, and mechanism ideas to be planned before physical manufacture.

The main advantage was control. Unlike 3D scanning, which captures an existing object, CAD allowed the design to be created intentionally. It also connected directly with 3D printing because the model could be exported as STL files. The limitation was that CAD alone could not prove material behaviour, component strength, or joint friction.

3D Printing

3D printing was chosen as the main physical prototyping method because it provided a direct route from virtual design to physical artefact. It allowed custom parts to be manufactured without moulds or complex tooling. This made it suitable for an educational prototype with limited time.

The limitation is that printed parts may not always be strong, light, or smooth enough for final use. Moving components may need redesign, sanding, stronger material, or different print orientation. For this project, 3D printing was suitable for early physical validation rather than a complete production-ready solution.

Spandex Wing Skin

Spandex was selected as the preferred wing skin because it is lightweight and flexible. This makes it more suitable for a Pterodactyl-style membrane than a fully rigid printed wing. It also reduces the load that a future servo mechanism would need to move.

The limitation is that spandex depends on correct tensioning. Too loose, and the wing loses shape; too tight, and it may resist motion. A future version would need a frame or rib structure to hold the spandex in the correct shape while still allowing smooth wing movement.

Servo Actuation

Servo actuation was selected as the preferred future mechanism because it provides controlled angular movement. This is suitable for wing flapping because the wing needs to move between defined positions. Compared with a continuous DC motor, a servo is easier to control for a small demonstration prototype.

The limitation is torque. The servo must be strong enough to move the wing structure and overcome friction and material resistance. Therefore, future testing should compare servo strength, wing length, linkage geometry, and pivot design before the mechanism is built into the final body.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was considered as an alternative method for capturing organic dinosaur-like forms. It would be useful if a clay, foam, or hand-sculpted Pterodactyl body already existed and needed to be converted into a digital model.

It was not selected because the project was designed digitally from the beginning. There was no existing physical model to scan. In wider industry, 3D scanning is useful for reverse engineering, inspection, custom-fit products, and heritage preservation, but for this project CAD was more suitable.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was considered in relation to wing movement. It could be useful in a more advanced version if the aim was to compare the prototype’s wing movement with real bird or bat motion.

It was not selected for the current stage because the planned movement was a simple servo-actuated flap, not a detailed biological motion study. Motion capture would have added complexity without directly improving the first physical prototype.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was considered for flat support pieces, rails, wing ribs, or lightweight internal frame components. It is accurate, repeatable, and useful for sheet materials.

It was not selected as the main method because the Pterodactyl form and wing components required more organic 3D geometry. However, laser cutting could be useful in a future version for producing a lightweight frame under the spandex wing skin.

Resin Production and Composite Methods

Resin production and composite methods were considered as future development options. Resin could improve surface detail for the head or body, while composites could create lightweight and strong wing structures.

They were not selected for the current stage because they require more preparation, post-processing, and workshop control. For the first development iteration, CAD and 3D printing were more direct and practical.

Haptic Sculpting

Haptic sculpting was considered as a way to create more organic dinosaur features such as the head, body curves, or skin texture. It could be useful because a Pterodactyl is not purely geometric.

It was not selected as the main method because the current stage focused on wing geometry, mechanism layout, and manufacturable parts. Haptic sculpting would be more suitable in a later aesthetic refinement stage once the functional structure has been tested.

Final Chosen Workflow

After comparing the available options, the selected workflow was: CAD and 3D modelling → STL component development → 3D printing → spandex wing skin selection → physical evaluation → proposed servo actuation for a future functional iteration.

This was chosen because it matched the current prototype stage. CAD and 3D modelling supported controlled virtual development. 3D printing created the first physical evidence. Spandex was selected for the future wing membrane because of its low weight and flexibility. Servo actuation was selected as the most suitable next step for controlled wing movement.

Reflection and Future Development

What Worked Well

CAD and 3D modelling worked well because they gave the group a clear visual direction and supported the creation of STL files. The project had a visible route from virtual design into physical prototyping.

Main Limitations

The current stage does not yet include a complete working servo wing system. The mechanism still needs physical testing for torque, pivot friction, wing load, and spandex tension.

Future Improvements

A future version should test the servo and wing linkage separately before final assembly. The wing frame, spandex membrane, and printed mechanism parts should then be refined together.

This project helped me understand that product development is not always a straight path from idea to final working model. The CAD model made the Pterodactyl concept visible, but the physical 3D printed component gave more useful feedback about scale, form, and manufacturability. This showed me that virtual prototypes and physical prototypes should support each other rather than be treated as separate tasks.

The selected workflow was appropriate because it matched the current development stage. CAD and 3D modelling allowed the design to be controlled, STL files prepared the parts for manufacturing, and 3D printing produced physical evidence. Spandex and servo actuation were then selected as the most suitable future material and movement choices. This made the workflow realistic rather than overcomplicated.

If I developed the project further, I would first create a small test rig for the servo mechanism instead of immediately integrating it into the full body. This would allow the wing load, pivot friction, linkage length, and spandex tension to be tested separately. After that, the mechanism could be integrated into the Pterodactyl body with more confidence.

The Pterodactyl prototype reached a valuable early product development stage. The project produced CAD designs, mechanism layouts, STL files, and a 3D printed component, while also selecting spandex and servo actuation for a future functional version. From my individual perspective, the most important learning outcome was understanding how different prototyping methods should be selected based on suitability, not simply because they are available. The project demonstrates a clear route from virtual design to physical prototyping and provides a strong foundation for later wing actuation and robotic skin development.
