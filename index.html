From Virtual Model to Physical Prototype:
Pterodactyl Robotic Skin Development

This portfolio presents my individual reflection on a group project to develop a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype. The Product Development Journey

Project Overview and Individual Context

A group project presented through my own reflective product development narrative.

The group project aimed to develop a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype. The concept was chosen because the Pterodactyl has a clear connection to wings, membranes and movement, making it suitable for robotic modelling and drone skin design.

At the time of writing, the prototype is in the final printing and preparation stage. This portfolio therefore presents the artefact as a structured preproduction prototype rather than a finished production model. Evidence includes Blender/CAD designs, mechanism layouts, STL files, PETG printing, risk assessment, skin planning and wing movement research.

My individual role focused on researching wing movement. I investigated motors, servo actuation, strings, elastic thread and material tension. Motorised movement was initially the main direction, but budget and time constraints made string-assisted movement more practical for the current prototype. Servo actuation remains the preferred future improvement.

Product Development Process

The project was structured around concept design, design development, detailed development, preproduction and future production planning.

1. Concept Design

High-level ideation, selecting the Pterodactyl form and identifying the wing movement challenge.

2. Design Development

Blender/CAD modelling, mechanism layout, material comparison and movement options.

3. Detailed Development

STL preparation, print planning, skin seams, adhesive points and access panels.

4. Preproduction

PETG 3D printing, risk assessment, post-processing and physical prototype evaluation.

5. Production Plan

Future skin application, string or servo testing, refinement and final presentation.

1. Concept Design

The first phase was high-level ideation. The Pterodactyl was selected because it gave the project a recognisable form and a clear wing movement challenge. From my perspective, the main question was how the wings could move while still looking like a realistic membrane.

Phase 1: Concept Design

High-level ideation and selection of the Pterodactyl concept.

The group selected a Pterodactyl because its wings created a clear opportunity to explore robotic skin, flexible membranes and movement. Alternatives included a static display model, manual wing movement, motorised wings, servo-controlled wings and string-assisted wings. A flexible wing concept was chosen because it connected more strongly to the module theme than a fully rigid display model.

Phase 2: Design Development

Connecting the concept with mechanical design, cost and material decisions.

Blender/CAD was used because the Pterodactyl form needed organic shaping and digital control before manufacture. Movement options were also compared. A DC motor could provide rotation, but it would need a crank or linkage to create flapping. A servo was more suitable because it can move between controlled angles. However, due to time and budget limits, string-assisted movement became the practical current option.

Material alternatives were also considered. PLA was easy to print but more brittle. ABS could be stronger but is harder to print reliably. PETG was selected because it is easier to print than ABS, less brittle than PLA, and more suitable for a handled prototype.

Phase 3: Detailed Development

Preparing the design for fabrication and future maintenance.

Detailed development focused on STL preparation, print supports, overhangs and post-processing. The skin plan also became more detailed. A fully sealed skin was rejected because it would make the internal mechanism difficult to access. Selective adhesive points, seams, hidden screws or magnet panels were preferred because they would allow maintenance without destroying the skin.

The wing membrane was planned as fabric or spandex with a thin silicone layer. This offered a balance between flexibility, realism and access. Extra fabric could be added at stress points if testing showed weakness.

Phase 4: Preproduction

Physical samples, PETG printing and risk assessment.

The preproduction phase moved the project from digital design into physical sampling. The PETG print provides evidence of the virtual-to-physical transition and allows the design to be judged for scale, surface quality, support structure, strength, sharp edges and post-processing needs.

A risk assessment was also included because physical prototyping introduces hazards such as hot printer parts, fumes, damaged cables, sharp tools, support removal, trip hazards and sharp edges on the finished model. This showed me that physical prototyping requires safety planning as well as design planning.

Phase 5: Production and Future Production Planning

Using the current prototype as a foundation for a more functional version.

The production phase is presented as a future plan rather than a completed final product. A future version would include support removal, sanding, skin application, string or servo movement testing, access panel refinement and final colour detailing. Grey, brown and dull green colours were considered for a more natural skin finish.

Final Selected Workflow

Blender/CAD design → mechanism and skin planning → STL preparation → PETG 3D printing → risk assessment and post-processing → spandex/fabric membrane with silicone planning → string movement for the current prototype → servo actuation as a future improvement.

Skin, Membrane and Movement

Skin and Wing Membrane Design

Developing the outer skin strategy for realism, flexibility and access.

The skin design was important because the project was not only a rigid dinosaur shape. Silicone was considered for a realistic flexible surface, while foam or a similar material could smooth difficult body areas and round rough edges.

The wing membrane was planned as fabric or spandex with a thin silicone layer. Fabric or spandex provides a lightweight flexible base, while silicone improves realism. Adhesive would be applied only at selected points, because applying it everywhere would reduce access and may make the membrane too stiff.

The skin plan includes possible adhesive points, seam positions, hidden screws or magnetic panels. These decisions were important because the internal mechanism would need to remain accessible for testing, adjustment and future servo integration.

Skin Design Decision

The preferred skin strategy was a flexible membrane system using fabric or spandex, selective adhesive points and a thin silicone surface layer. Extra reinforcement could be added at stress points if testing showed weakness.

String, Thread and Actuation Method Selection

Comparing movement options and selecting a practical current method.

My individual contribution focused on wing movement. I compared a DC motor, servo motor, strings, shirring elastic thread, thin elastic cord, lycra/spandex elastic thread, thin rubber cord and normal sewing thread.

A DC motor could provide rotation, but it would need extra linkage to create flapping. A servo was more suitable for controlled wing movement because it can rotate between set angles. However, due to budget and time constraints, string-assisted movement became more practical for the current prototype.

I proposed spandex elastic thread because the wing skin was also planned to use spandex. Since both materials are stretchy, they should move together more naturally. The key limitation is tension: too tight could resist movement, while too loose could lose the wing shape.

Critical Appraisal

Techniques Used, Considered and Rejected

Each method is appraised for suitability in this project and wider industrial product development.

Blender, CAD and 3D Modelling

Blender/CAD was selected because the Pterodactyl needed controlled digital development before manufacture. Blender was useful for organic body forms, while CAD-style modelling helped with structure and fabrication planning.

The limitation is that digital models cannot fully prove print quality, strength, friction or handling. In industry, CAD and 3D modelling are essential for visualisation, revision, communication and manufacturing preparation.

PETG 3D Printing

3D printing was selected because it gave a direct route from digital model to physical artefact. PETG was chosen because it is easier to print than ABS, less brittle than PLA, and offers good impact resistance, temperature resistance and layer adhesion.

The limitation is that printed parts still need support removal, post-processing and possible redesign. In industry, 3D printing is valuable for rapid prototyping and custom components, but material choice and print orientation are important.

Skin and Membrane Design

Spandex, fabric and silicone were considered for the skin and wing membrane. Spandex/fabric was suitable because it is lightweight and flexible, while silicone could improve realism.

A fully sealed silicone skin was not ideal because it would block maintenance access. Selective adhesive points, seams and possible magnetic panels were preferred. In industry, outer casing must protect and improve appearance without preventing repair.

String-Assisted Movement and Servo Actuation

A DC motor was considered but would require extra mechanisms to create flapping. A servo was preferred for future movement because it can move between set angles.

For the current prototype, string-assisted movement was more realistic due to cost and time. The limitation is that string tension must be controlled carefully. In robotics, cable-driven and servo-driven mechanisms both require testing for load, friction and repeatability.

3D Scanning

3D scanning was considered but not selected. It would be useful if a clay, foam or hand-made Pterodactyl body needed to be converted into a digital model.

It was less suitable because the design started digitally. In industry, 3D scanning is useful for reverse engineering, inspection, medical modelling, heritage work and custom-fit products.

Motion Capture

Motion capture was considered in relation to wing movement, but it was not needed for a simple string or servo-assisted flap.

It could be useful in a future version to compare the prototype with real bird or bat wing motion. In industry, motion capture is used in robotics, animation, biomechanics and movement analysis.

Haptic Sculpting

Haptic sculpting could help create organic details such as wrinkles, skin texture or body curves.

It was not selected because the current stage focused on manufacturable geometry, mechanism layout and 3D printing. It would be better for later aesthetic refinement.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting was considered for flat support pieces, rails or wing ribs. It is accurate and repeatable for sheet materials.

It was not selected as the main method because the Pterodactyl required organic 3D geometry. It could be useful later for a lightweight internal frame under the spandex membrane.

Resin Production and Composite Methods

Resin and composites were considered for future development. Resin could improve detail and surface finish, while composites could create lighter and stronger structures.

They were not selected because they require more post-processing and workshop control. For this stage, PETG 3D printing was more direct and practical.

Critical Appraisal Summary

Blender/CAD, STL preparation and PETG 3D printing were selected because they best supported the current virtual-to-physical stage. Spandex, silicone, seams and string movement supported a practical skin and movement strategy. Other techniques were appraised as alternatives or future options.

Individual Reflection

What Worked Well

The virtual-to-physical route was clear: Blender/CAD created the design, STL files prepared it for fabrication, and PETG printing produced physical evidence.

Main Limitations

Full movement testing is not yet complete. String tension, servo torque, spandex behaviour, seam access and post-processing still need practical testing.

Future Improvements

A future version should test a small wing rig first, then refine the movement system, membrane tension, access panels and final skin finish.

This project showed me that product development does not move directly from idea to final product. The virtual model gave a clear design direction, while the physical print revealed scale, support, surface and handling issues.

My movement research showed that actuation is not only about choosing a motor. Wing material, string tension, joint friction, cost, time and access all affect whether movement is realistic.

The skin plan showed that a prototype can fail if the mechanism becomes inaccessible. Selective adhesive points, seams, possible magnetic panels and reinforced stress points were more practical than permanently sealing the skin.

Next time, I would print smaller test pieces first, build a small wing rig, compare string and servo movement, test spandex tension, refine the CAD model, then apply silicone only after access and movement had been confirmed.

The project reached a valuable preproduction prototype stage. It produced Blender/CAD models, mechanism layouts, STL files, PETG printing evidence, risk assessment work and skin planning. My main learning was that prototyping methods should be selected based on suitability, constraints and future development potential.
