An individual reflection on developing a Pterodactyl-inspired robotic skin prototype.

Why We Chose a Pterodactyl:
Skin, Structure and Wing Movement

Our group discussed several possible ideas before selecting the Pterodactyl. I felt this direction fitted the assignment because its wings naturally connected to robotic skin, movement and physical prototyping. It allowed me to explore flexible membrane materials, string routing, roller-guided movement and future servo actuation. This individual reflection portfolio explains what I explored, what decisions made sense, and what I learned during the process.

1. Concept Design

Choosing a concept that supported skin, structure and movement.

At the concept design stage, different creature and movement ideas were discussed before the Pterodactyl direction was selected. I understood it to be a strong choice because it created clear development challenges: large wings, a flexible membrane, an outer skin, internal support and possible movement.

The useful comparison at this stage was between different ways of achieving movement and skin behaviour. A fully rigid printed wing could make the structure easier to control, but it would not represent the flexible membrane of a Pterodactyl very well. A flexible membrane was more suitable because it connected the creature concept with robotic skin design and future wing movement.

My Reflection

This stage helped me understand that a good concept should support the technical aims of the assignment. The Pterodactyl was suitable because its wings created questions about movement, membrane behaviour, skin attachment, material tension and future actuation. This made the concept useful for exploring the route from virtual design to physical prototype.

2. Product Development

Turning the concept into a practical prototype direction.

Product development was where the project became more practical. The work covered Blender/CAD modelling, 3D printing material choice, skin research, risk planning and wing movement. My main contribution focused on motors, servo actuation, strings and elastic thread options, while the wider design decisions helped me understand how each part affected the prototype.

I realised that the body, skin and movement system could not be treated separately. If the wing membrane was too stiff, the movement system would need more force. If the string path was poorly aligned, the wing could move unevenly. If the skin was fixed too permanently, the internal mechanism would become difficult to adjust.

Blender/CAD Modelling

Blender Body Development Video

This video shows the digital model being reviewed before moving towards physical manufacturing.

Blender/CAD modelling was used to develop the Pterodactyl body and wing form before physical manufacture. The advantage was that the shape could be viewed, adjusted and prepared digitally before printing. CAD and 3D modelling are widely used to visualise, revise and prepare designs before manufacturing (Autodesk, n.d.).

Other digital routes were considered. 3D scanning would be useful if a hand-made model already existed and needed to be converted into a digital file. Haptic sculpting could help with natural skin texture and wrinkles. However, the project began digitally, so the priority was producing a manufacturable form that could move towards 3D printing and mechanism planning.

My Reflection

Reviewing the digital design helped me understand why virtual prototyping matters. It allowed the body shape, wing layout and possible mechanism space to be considered before printing. However, a digital model alone cannot prove whether movement, material tension or post-processing will work in reality.

Movement Development: String, Roller Routing and Future Servo Actuation

The movement idea started with the need to move the wings in a simple but controllable way. A string-based system was explored because it could transfer pulling force to the wings without making the prototype too complex. As the idea developed, rollers became important because they could guide the string through the body more smoothly and reduce friction. Recent work on flapping-wing robots also shows that wing movement involves challenges in actuation, prototyping and control, so a simple movement idea still needs careful testing (Rafee Nekoo et al., 2025).

A DC motor was considered as one possible actuator, but it would need a crank or linkage to convert continuous rotation into a wing-flapping motion. A servo motor was more suitable for a future version because it can move between set angles, matching the upstroke and downstroke of a wing. The stronger idea was that a future servo could pull or control the string through the guided roller path, rather than forcing the wing directly.

Several string-routing ideas were considered, including the eye system, locking cuff method and roller system. The eye system could keep the string in position, but it may create rubbing at the guide points. The locking cuff could secure the string, but it may make tension adjustment harder. The roller system was selected because it gave the string a smoother path and could support future servo-controlled pulling. Cable-driven systems depend strongly on cable routing, tension, friction and control uncertainty, which supports why string path design mattered in this prototype (Jin et al., 2024).

My Reflection

This was one of my main learning points. At first, I focused mainly on the actuator, such as whether a motor or servo would be suitable. I then realised that the actuator is only one part of the system. The string path, roller position, friction, tension and attachment points all affect whether the wing can move smoothly.

Thread and Wing Support Options

I also looked into different thread and string options for supporting the wing membrane: shirring elastic thread, thin elastic cord, lycra/spandex elastic thread, thin rubber cord and normal sewing thread. Normal sewing thread would be simple to attach but has little stretch. Thin rubber cord provides stretch but may be harder to control neatly. Shirring elastic and thin elastic cord were possible options, but I felt spandex elastic thread matched the wing skin better.

I proposed spandex elastic thread because the wing skin was also planned to use spandex. Since both materials are stretchy, they should move together more naturally. The limitation is tension: if it is too tight, it could resist movement; if it is too loose, the wing may not hold its shape.

My Reflection

This helped me understand that small material choices can affect mechanical performance. The motor, servo, string and roller path are only part of movement. The thread, membrane and tension also decide whether the wing can move smoothly.

3D Printing Material Selection

PLA, ABS and PETG were compared for the printed prototype. PLA is easy to print but more brittle. ABS can be strong but usually needs more controlled printing conditions. PETG was selected because it offered a better balance between printability, toughness and durability. Recent comparison work on PLA, ABS and PET-G also supports that these materials differ significantly in strength behaviour, making material choice important for prototype performance (Stecuła et al., 2024).

My Reflection

Reviewing the material decision helped me understand that filament choice affects strength, handling, safety and post-processing, not just appearance.

Skin and Wing Membrane Planning

The project explored fabric, spandex and silicone-based skin ideas. Spandex was suitable for the wing membrane because it is lightweight and flexible. A thin silicone layer could improve realism, but if applied too heavily it could add stiffness, weight and access problems. Soft robotics literature also highlights that compliant materials can support flexible movement but introduce challenges around stiffness, motion precision and manufacturing (Ambaye, Boldsaikhan and Krishnan, 2024).

For this reason, selective adhesive points, seam lines, hidden screws or magnetic panels were considered more practical than permanently sealing the skin. This mattered because an early prototype needs to remain adjustable while movement and tension are still being tested.

My Reflection

The skin planning connected directly to my movement research because the wing material affects how hard the mechanism has to work. A realistic-looking skin that is too stiff could make movement unreliable.

3. Pre-Production

Moving from virtual design into physical printing and safety planning.

Pre-production involved preparing the design for physical manufacture. PETG 3D printing was selected because it allowed the digital model to become a physical prototype. Remaining only at the virtual stage would not reveal issues such as support structures, sharp edges, surface finish, post-processing needs or handling strength.

The advantage of 3D printing was that it created a real object that could be inspected and developed further. The limitation was that the printed part still required support removal, sanding and checking for weak or sharp areas. This showed me that printing is not the end of the process; it is the beginning of physical evaluation.

The risk assessment identified hazards linked to printer setup, heated parts, fumes, damaged cables, filament handling, support removal, sharp tools and finished model handling. This made the physical stage more realistic because it considered whether the prototype could be handled and developed safely.

Physical Prototype Video

This video supports the pre-production stage by showing the transition from digital design into a physical printed prototype.

My Reflection

This stage helped me understand the difference between a virtual prototype and a physical prototype. In Blender/CAD, the design can look complete, but the physical print reveals supports, edges, surface finish, strength, access and safety issues.

4. Post-Production and Future Development

Planning finishing, movement testing and the next functional version.

Post-production is a planned next step. The printed model would first need support removal, sanding and surface improvement. After that, the selected string and roller path should be tested on a smaller wing rig before the final skin is attached. This would allow string tension, roller alignment, membrane resistance and wing movement to be tested without risking damage to the full prototype.

A future servo could then be used to pull the guided string path more consistently. For the final finish, spandex or fabric could be attached first, followed by selective silicone coating if needed. Grey, brown and dull green tones were considered for a natural Pterodactyl appearance.

Final Selected Workflow

Blender/CAD design → mechanism and skin planning → STL preparation → PETG 3D printing → risk assessment and post-processing → spandex/fabric membrane planning → selected roller-guided string path for the current prototype → servo-controlled string actuation as a future improvement.

My Reflection

If I developed the project further, I would not apply the final skin immediately. I would first test the wing mechanism separately, then adjust the string path, roller alignment and membrane tension. This would reduce the risk of covering the mechanism too early and later damaging the skin to fix movement problems.

Critical Appraisal

Methods Used, Considered and Rejected

Critical appraisal of methods used and methods considered but not selected.

Blender, CAD and 3D Modelling

Blender/CAD was suitable because the Pterodactyl needed controlled digital development before manufacture. It supported organic shaping and fabrication planning. The limitation was that a digital model cannot prove print quality, joint friction, material tension or handling strength.

PETG 3D Printing

PETG 3D printing created a direct route from digital model to physical artefact. PETG was suitable because it balanced printability and durability. The limitation is that printed parts still need support removal, finishing and inspection.

String-Roller Movement and Future Servo Actuation

The movement approach was based on a string-guided system supported by rollers. The eye system and locking cuff method were considered, but the roller system gave a smoother string path and remained compatible with future servo-controlled string actuation. The limitation is that it still depends on accurate alignment, tension and secure attachment points.

Skin and Membrane Design

Spandex/fabric was suitable because it is lightweight and flexible. Silicone could improve realism, but it may add stiffness and access problems if applied too heavily. The selected direction was to keep the skin accessible using seams, selective adhesive points or removable panels.

3D Scanning

3D scanning would be useful if a physical clay or foam model had to be digitised. It was less suitable here because the project began digitally. In wider industry, scanning is useful for reverse engineering, inspection and custom-fit products.

Motion Capture

Motion capture could be useful in a future version to compare the wing path with biological movement. It was not necessary for the current prototype because the movement concept focused on a simple guided string path.

Haptic Sculpting

Haptic sculpting could support surface detail such as wrinkles and natural skin texture. For this stage, fabrication and movement planning were more important than detailed aesthetic refinement.

Laser Cutting

Laser cutting could be useful for flat ribs, rails or support pieces. It was not the main method because the Pterodactyl body required organic 3D geometry, making 3D printing more suitable.

Resin and Composite Methods

Resin could improve surface detail, while composites could create lighter structures. They were considered more suitable for a later refined version because they require more post-processing and workshop control.

Final Reflection

Key Learning

A final reflection on the main lessons from the development process.

This project helped me understand that a prototype is not only judged by whether it is finished, but by how clearly the development process is understood. The Blender/CAD model helped plan the form, but the PETG print revealed practical issues such as support removal, surface finish, sharp edges and access.

My biggest learning was that wing movement depends on more than the actuator. At first, I focused mainly on motors and servo movement, but I later realised that string routing, roller alignment, membrane tension, friction and access points all affect whether the wing can work reliably.

If I developed the project further, I would build a small wing test rig first. I would compare the selected string and roller system with servo-controlled string actuation, test the spandex membrane tension, and only apply the final silicone skin once the movement and access points were confirmed.
